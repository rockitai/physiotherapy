01The pelvic floor during pregnancy

Pregnancy is a significant period in terms of its effect on the abdominal and pelvic floor muscles. During this time, the pelvic floor and the abdominal wall are weakened by the pregnancy itself (the added mechanical load of the weight of the uterus and its contents, which the pelvic floor and abdomen have to carry) and by the presence of progesterone and relaxin = hormones whose job is to "soften" soft tissues, so that the tissues of the abdominal wall can "contain" the pregnancy and, when the time comes, the baby can pass through the pelvic floor. The connective tissues of the pelvic floor and the abdominal wall stretch, and may also tear (torn connective tissue can only be repaired surgically, if at all). In addition, the pressure on the pelvic floor nerves in the late stages of pregnancy, as they are squeezed between the baby's head and the mother's pelvic bone, causes them some damage that keeps them from returning to their original state (as before a first pregnancy and birth). The "rule" for the pelvic floor and the abdominal wall: the more pregnancies a woman has, with bigger babies, and the longer or more interventional the births (vacuum / forceps) – the greater the chance of pelvic floor damage.

  • Back pain in pregnancy has several causes (all together): 1. The body's center of gravity shifts because of the growing belly – more load falls on the spine. 2. In the background – hormones that soften all the ligaments and tissues in the body, so the supporting tissues are more flexible and give less stability. 3. The pelvic joints can also refer pain, which sometimes seems to come from the back (but really comes from the pelvic joints) – there too, this happens because of excess movement in joints that normally do not move much, against the background of the tissue-softening hormones. 4. The muscles of the stabilizing system (the abdominal and pelvic floor muscles) are weaker than their norm because they are not at their physiological length, and they too "enjoy" the softening hormones. As for pain that comes from the pelvic joints – the source of the pain is excess flexibility in the pelvic joints caused by the pregnancy hormones (relaxin and progesterone), whose job is to soften all the body's tissues to make room to "contain" the pregnancy (everything is pushed aside, the tissues stretch and so on). These hormones (which do not distinguish between the connective tissue of the abdomen and, say, the ligaments that stabilize the ankle…) also soften the pelvic joints (with good intentions… because then the pelvis can widen during birth and give the baby's head more room). This pain is very common among pregnant women, even from the very start of pregnancy (because the hormones are released from the very start). The excess mobility in the joints puts more strain on the ligaments that stabilize the joint, and these cause a lot of pain, which can sometimes even radiate along the whole leg and mislead us into thinking it is a herniated disc or something similar. Pelvic joint pain has no consequences for the course of the birth, except that there is sometimes more sensitivity after the birth, but it heals slowly after the birth.

  • There are physiotherapy techniques for treating the pelvic joints during pregnancy (this is an individual treatment). These techniques encourage the muscular system that supports the pelvic joints, and can bring significant relief. Other things that can help: a pelvic belt. In daily behavior: avoid (as far as possible) actions that put shearing forces on the pelvic joints – actions with one leg here and one leg there, such as climbing stairs; sleep with a pillow between your legs (it reduces the load on the pelvic joints). In cases of very severe pain, when getting around (walking) is really difficult, a walker may be considered to take the load off the joints, or in very, very severe cases even a wheelchair for getting around.

  • Symphysiolysis is excess mobility of the pubic symphysis, the front joint of the pelvic ring, caused by the pregnancy hormones (relaxin and progesterone), which soften the ligaments and tissues that stabilize the joint (and all the other joints in the body too). This pain in the groin, or pain in the back pelvic joints, is very common during pregnancy. The guidance for easing the pain appears under "Pain in the pelvic joints and back".

  • The pelvic floor should be exercised every day, all your life, regardless of pregnancy. Strengthening the pelvic floor before pregnancy does not give you an "insurance policy" against problems. But if you are aware of your pelvic floor and exercise it, you will arrive with more skilled and stronger muscles, so even if it weakens, at least your starting point was better. A woman who exercises routinely finds it easier to recognize the muscle's action and to start exercising it again with relative ease after the birth. Pelvic floor strength also helps the birth itself, because the baby makes its internal rotation on the inner muscle layer on its way out, and if this muscle layer is weak and slack, the baby will find it harder to do this and may need interventional help (vacuum, forceps).

  • In principle there is no problem with exercising the pelvic floor and postural muscles during pregnancy – quite the opposite: during pregnancy and around the birth it is very important, because of the weakening the pelvic floor goes through under the influence of the pregnancy hormones and of the birth itself. Of course, if for some reason your doctor has forbidden exercise (rare), follow their instructions. The earlier you learn to recognize and work with the muscles, the better – because after the birth the area will weaken significantly further, and as a rule, the weaker the muscles you bring to rehabilitation, the harder they are to rehabilitate. You will also get guidance on a healthy lifestyle and correct use of the body, to immediately reduce damage you may be causing your body (for example, straining on the toilet), as well as guidance in preparing for birth – teaching you all the movements and positions whose aim, among other things, is to protect the pelvic floor during birth.

  • Urinary leakage during pregnancy and after birth is not rare, and sadly it is even quite common. What is even more common, and even sadder, is that women are embarrassed to raise it with their doctor, and sometimes, even when they do, they are not referred for treatment of the root of the problem. First, it is important to understand that the pelvic floor is already weakened by the p-r-e-g-n-a-n-c-y itself, for two main reasons. One: the presence of progesterone and relaxin – hormones whose job is to "soften" soft tissues (among other things, so that when the time comes the baby can pass through the pelvic floor). Progesterone is present afterwards too – during breastfeeding – so women who breastfeed are exposed to its effects for longer (this does not mean, heaven forbid, that you should not breastfeed, but be aware that the pelvic floor needs more attention while you are breastfeeding). It is true that the situation often "improves" after birth, but this is usually because of the different hormonal balance in the body, not because the root of the problem was treated by strengthening the pelvic floor. The second reason: the mechanical load (the weight of the pregnancy) on the pelvic floor. – So mothers who give birth by "cesarean" are not completely exempt either… And of course, afterwards each birth adds its own "contribution" to the cumulative damage.

  • Perineal massage – for perineal massage to be effective (effective = widening the perineum so that the chance of a perineal cut during birth is reduced), you need an effective stretch that works on the collagen component of the muscle tissue. Such a stretch is hard to achieve with home massage, where the partner does the stretching for the mother, because most people who are not professionals find it hard to feel where the limit of the stretch is, and so on. Also, some men shy away from the somewhat gynecological "role" of being "the one who stretches" – unless the perineal massage comes as part of sexual play, in a good spirit that suits both partners. A mother trying to massage herself has a hard time, because she cannot truly relax: the position she needs to reach the area physically does not let her be relaxed. In short – not impossible, but complicated. My personal recommendation is the "Epi-No" device, which lets the mother work on her own, at her own pace, without depending on a partner; it works on a circumferential stretch (which cannot be achieved by hand); and (in my eyes this is the device's real strength) it lets the mother practice pushing correctly – that is, learn to relax the pelvic floor muscles together with the right timing of the transverse abdominal muscle, while something uncomfortable is in the vagina.

  • The Epi-No device helps a great deal in learning to relax and to push correctly. How do you work with the Epi-No? – Insert the Epi-No balloon into the vagina, inflated just a tiny bit (so that it is slightly firm). Inflate until you feel a s-t-r-e-t-c-h, but n-o (!) pain and n-o (!) burning. Leave it for about 3 minutes, practicing relaxation during that time (because when there is something unpleasant in the vagina, the muscle responds with a protective contraction). After 3 minutes, inflate a little more, again until you feel a stretch but no burning and no pain, and so learn to relax (when you relax, you see the pressure drop on the pressure gauge). After another 3 minutes, inflate a little again until you feel a stretch, and finally practice pushing the balloon out while relaxing, and while giving the balloon counter-support with your hand (because the moment it starts to move, the protective contraction against the discomfort will come again). In this way you bring it out very gradually, keeping the pelvic floor relaxed, breathing out, and pushing with the transverse abdominal muscle.

  • There is no problem at all with using the Epi-No to exercise the pelvic floor; on the contrary, it can be a helpful aid (this refers to the device with the pressure gauge). It is just important to make sure first that you really know which contraction is meant. This is especially true if your pelvic floor muscles are already weak, since then their action is harder to recognize, and it is also best to allow longer rests between contractions and between sessions, which suits training a weak muscle better. Why is it important to know how to recognize it? – So as not to get the action wrong and push outward instead of contracting (the Epi-No responds and shows a rise in pressure even when you do the wrong action).

02Recovering and strengthening the pelvic floor after birth

During birth the pelvic floor is exposed to significant pressure and stretching. Sometimes spontaneous tears or a deliberate cut (episiotomy) are added, which make it lose even more of its strength. The postpartum period, and even several months after it, which make up the pelvic floor's recovery time from pregnancy and birth, are critically important for a woman's uro-gynecological future. The postpartum period is usually taken to be 6 weeks after the birth, but in practice the recovery time of the various pelvic floor tissues is much longer. The literature today speaks of about two months of recovery for the nerve, three months of recovery for the muscle, and about another four to six months

Key points about returning to activity after the birth:

  1. 01

    Pelvic floor exercises right after birth

    Avoid straining when passing urine or stool as far as possible. To ease pelvic floor pain when passing urine or stool (because of stitches and so on), you can support the pelvic floor tissues with the palm of your hand (with toilet paper or a clean cloth on it).

    Start exercising and using the pelvic floor muscles right after the birth: contract the muscles while breathing out, as a first step before any effort you make (coughing, sneezing, lifting things or the baby and so on), and when you return to exercise, let the pelvic floor "set the pace" – that is, if during an exercise you feel the pelvic floor cannot stay contracted, stop the exercise. Recovery of the connective tissues (in women who breastfeed this takes even longer, because of the presence of progesterone in the body).

  2. 02

    Returning to sex after the birth

    In the period after birth, because of low estrogen levels, sexual arousal takes longer: engorgement of the labia and vagina is slower, and the vagina is less lubricated. As long as the woman is breastfeeding this stays relevant, and on top of it comes a suppression of sexual desire because of the hormone oxytocin, which is released during breastfeeding and creates a feeling of calm and suppresses desire. Besides the low estrogen levels, there are sometimes also "souvenirs" of the birth: scars that healed with adhesions to nearby tissues, or healed prominently. When there is pelvic floor pain, the pelvic floor muscles respond with a protective contraction – which can make the pain worse because there is more friction, and can even make penetration really difficult. Pain during sex can be helped with lubricants such as KY gel (sold at any pharmacy or sex shop). When there is pain from problematic scarring of the stitches, or a muscle contraction that does not allow penetration, physiotherapy techniques can help release and soften scars and relax the muscles. Vaginal widening after birth may come from weakened muscles and also from damage to the connective tissues (or both…). This feeling of widening should lessen with exercise and as the area recovers, assuming there was no massive damage to the connective tissues.

  3. 03

    If there is urinary leakage right after the birth

    It is worth knowing that in most cases, a complete loss of control settles and improves within a few days. The reason you lose the ability to control urine right after the birth is the trauma that the nerves supplying the pelvic floor muscles go through during the birth (and of course, the longer the birth, with prolonged pushing and so on, the greater the damage). After the birth, each component of the pelvic floor begins to recover – the muscles, the nerves and the connective tissues. The less swollen and the more "active" the area (= using the muscles), the easier it is for the nerves to recover and for control to return. What to do in the meantime? a. Try to do pelvic floor contractions – you can try the stop test, and you can also try contracting the anus if that contraction is easier for you to recognize right now. b. Avoid straining on the toilet at all costs (do not pass stool or urine while straining). c. Avoid effort (as far as possible – do not lift heavy things, let others carry for you, and if you have to lift, try to contract the pelvic floor before and during the lift). d. Try to reduce swelling in the area, both with cold pads soaked in magnesium ("Epsom salt") and by trying to do contractions, which encourage the swelling to be absorbed, better blood flow and faster healing. e. Avoid sitting on a "swim ring" – (grandmothers used to bring a ring for the new mother so that she could sit without her pelvic floor touching the seat, to ease the pain of the stitches) – if the stitches hurt, sit on a soft but even surface = that is, do not leave the pelvic floor "hanging" in the air without support.

03Pelvic floor tips for the period after birth

  • 01Be especially careful to exercise and use your pelvic floor every day.
  • 02Get up and lie down correctly (by rolling onto your side) to reduce the use of the long abdominal muscles.
  • 03After the birth – start exercising the pelvic floor muscles. This will help the nerves recover faster, help the stitches heal faster and help absorb swelling from the area.
  • 04Avoid sitting on the toilet for long, and especially avoid straining (to pass urine or stool).
  • 05If you are in pain (from stitches, swelling and so on), sit on a soft, even surface (for example a cushion), not on a ring. If it is hard to pass urine or stool because of pain, use the palm of your hand, wrapped in toilet paper, to support the pelvic floor while emptying. This lets you empty with much less pain.
  • 06During the postpartum period (the first 6 weeks after birth), avoid effort as much as you can, and if you do have to make an effort, be sure to contract the pelvic floor before and during it.
  • 07After 6 weeks (8 weeks after a cesarean) – return gradually to your usual physical activity, letting the pelvic floor "set" the pace of your return.
